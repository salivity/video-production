How D-Tap and V-Mount Power Camera Accessories

V-mount battery systems and D-Tap connections serve as the centralized power hub for modern video production rigs. By replacing individual, proprietary batteries for each device, a single high-capacity V-mount brick can simultaneously supply reliable direct current (DC) power to a camera body, external monitor, wireless video transmitter, follow-focus motor, and on-camera audio gear. This article breaks down how V-mount mounting plates and D-Tap distribution systems route, regulate, and safely deliver power to multiple camera accessories at the same time.

The Foundation: The V-Mount Battery Hub

A V-mount battery is a standardized, high-capacity lithium-ion power pack featuring a wedge-shaped locking mechanism that securely slides into a matching V-lock plate. Operating at a nominal voltage of 14.4V to 14.8V (which fluctuates between roughly 12V fully discharged and 16.8V fully charged), these batteries provide substantial watt-hour (Wh) ratings—often between 95Wh and 290Wh.

In a multi-accessory setup, the V-mount battery rarely connects directly to the camera alone. Instead, it docks onto a specialized power distribution plate mounted to the camera's 15mm or 19mm rail system. This plate acts as an electrical breakout board, drawing raw power from the battery terminals and routing it through various built-in outputs.

The Mechanism of D-Tap

D-Tap (also known as P-Tap or Power-Tap) is a two-pin connector design that delivers DC power directly from the battery cells. Most modern V-mount batteries feature one or more D-Tap ports integrated directly into the battery casing, while V-mount adapter plates typically add several more.

Because the D-Tap interface is simple, rugged, and compact, it has become the universal industry standard for powering third-party production gear. A standard D-Tap port outputs the battery’s unregulated nominal voltage (14.4V–14.8V) at currents typically rated up to 10 amps, providing more than enough overhead to drive multiple peripheral devices at once.

Simultaneous Multi-Device Routing

To power several accessories simultaneously from one V-mount source, filmmakers rely on three primary routing methods:

  1. Multi-Port Battery Plates: Advanced V-mount plates feature an array of outputs alongside the main battery terminal, including multiple D-Tap ports, USB-A/USB-C PD ports, 2-pin LEMO-style connectors, and regulated DC barrel jacks (such as 8V or 12V). This allows a camera, a monitor, and an audio receiver to plug directly into the plate simultaneously.
  2. On-Battery Ports: Many modern compact V-mount batteries include built-in D-Tap and high-wattage USB-C Power Delivery ports. This allows operators to power an accessory like an external monitor via D-Tap and recharge or power a camera or mobile device via USB-C straight from the battery itself, even without an external plate.
  3. D-Tap Splitters and Breakout Hubs: When a rig requires more connections than the plate or battery provides, operators plug a D-Tap multi-splitter (such as a 1-to-4 breakout cable) into a single D-Tap port. This single port then feeds power to multiple low-draw devices concurrently, such as a wireless video transmitter, a motorized lens controller, and an LED micro-light.

Voltage Regulation and Safety

Powering multiple devices simultaneously requires careful attention to voltage compatibility and electrical limits:

  • Regulated vs. Unregulated Lines: Devices like professional monitors and wireless transmitters often accept a wide input voltage range (typically 7V to 18V or higher), making them safe to run on unregulated 14.4V D-Tap lines. More sensitive devices, such as DSLR or mirrorless camera bodies expecting 7.4V–8.4V, require regulated dummy batteries or step-down converters built into the cable or plate to prevent internal circuitry damage.
  • Current Draw Budgets: Every V-mount battery has a maximum continuous discharge rating (usually between 10A and 15A). Powering a 30W camera, a 15W monitor, a 10W transmitter, and an 8W focus motor creates a combined draw of 63W. At nominal 14.4V, this equates to roughly 4.4 amps—well within the continuous discharge limit of a standard cinema battery.
  • Polarity Awareness: While D-Tap connectors are shaped to prevent incorrect insertion, forcing a connector backward can reverse the polarity and destroy connected accessories. Many modern distribution hubs include reverse-polarity protection, integrated fuses, and status LEDs to ensure that power is delivered safely across every branch of the rig.
