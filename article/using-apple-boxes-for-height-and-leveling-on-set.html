Using Apple Boxes for Height and Leveling on Set

On a video production set, achieving the perfect frame often requires micro-adjustments to the physical environment. An apple box set is an essential grip tool designed specifically to resolve height discrepancies, level uneven equipment, and quickly adjust camera perspectives. This article breaks down the standard components of an apple box set and explains how crew members utilize these versatile wooden boxes to solve common height and leveling issues efficiently during production.

The Standard Apple Box Set

A standard apple box set consists of four modular wooden boxes, each precisely dimensioned to stack, combine, or use individually:

  • Full Apple: 8" × 13" × 20"
  • Half Apple: 4" × 13" × 20"
  • Quarter Apple: 2" × 13" × 20"
  • Pancake (Eighth): 1" × 13" × 20"

Because these measurements are standardized, grips can combine them in any configuration to create exact elevations ranging from one inch to several feet.

Balancing Actor Heights and Eye Lines

One of the most frequent challenges on set is matching the heights of actors during two-shots or over-the-shoulder coverage. If one actor is significantly shorter than their scene partner, fitting both faces into a balanced frame can compromise composition.

Placing a full, half, or pancake apple box under the shorter actor—referred to on set as "manning up"—instantly raises their eyeline. This eliminates unnatural camera tilts, maintains proper headroom, and ensures the depth of field and framing remain consistent across reverse angles.

Leveling Camera Rigs and Dolly Track

Uneven terrain, whether on an outdoor location or an imperfect studio floor, disrupts camera movement and framing. Apple boxes provide a stable, solid base to level out support systems.

When laying dolly track over sloped or undulating ground, grips place apple boxes underneath the track supports, often paired with wooden wedges, to create a completely flat plane. For static shots on stairs or rocky surfaces, an apple box placed under one or two tripod legs instantly levels the camera head without forcing operators to adjust individual tripod leg extensions.

Fine-Tuning Props and Furniture

Standard furniture often fails to align with camera angles. A table may sit too low in the frame, or a chair might cause an actor to sink below their co-star's eyeline. Placing pancakes or quarter apples under table legs or seating elevates the scene elements to optimal frame height without looking unnatural on camera.

Providing Low-Angle Camera Platforms

When a shot requires a low angle, tripods—even baby legs—can sometimes be too tall or too wide to fit in tight spaces. A high-hat or a camera plate can be mounted directly onto an apple box, positioning the lens just inches off the floor while maintaining a rigid, vibration-free platform.
