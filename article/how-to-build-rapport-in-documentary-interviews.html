How to Build Rapport in Documentary Interviews

Building rapport with non-professional subjects is an essential skill in documentary filmmaking that directly influences the authenticity and emotional depth of a story. Unlike trained actors or media-trained experts, everyday people often feel intimidated by cameras, bright lights, and production crews. This article outlines practical strategies interviewers use to ease anxiety, establish genuine human connections, and create a safe environment where non-professional subjects feel comfortable sharing their personal truths on camera.

Connect Before the Cameras Roll

The foundation of rapport is built long before the record button is pressed. Interviewers should spend time talking with the subject off-camera, either during a pre-interview phone call or over a casual cup of coffee on the shoot day. Use this time to learn about their personality and boundaries without digging too deep into the core emotional material you want to capture on film. Showing genuine curiosity about their everyday life signals that you view them as a human being rather than just a narrative tool.

Demystify the Production Environment

A film set can feel overwhelming to someone who has never been on one. To reduce anxiety, briefly introduce the subject to the crew and explain what the equipment does. Walk them through the process simply: explain how the microphones work, clarify where they should look (typically at the interviewer, not down the lens), and explicitly remind them that documentary filming is not a live broadcast. Emphasize that if they stumble over their words, get nervous, or need a moment to collect their thoughts, they can pause and start again because the footage will be edited.

Ditch the Script for a Conversation

Holding a clipboard or rigidly reading from a list of questions immediately creates an artificial barrier. Skilled interviewers memorize their thematic goals and treat the interview as an organic conversation rather than an interrogation. Start with simple, low-stakes questions about their background to get them comfortable speaking in full sentences. Transition gradually to deeper topics, using their previous answers to shape the next question. This demonstrates that you are actively listening rather than merely waiting for your turn to speak.

Master Non-Verbal Encouragement

In video production, the interviewer must remain quiet while the subject is talking to preserve clean audio. Because you cannot use verbal affirmations like "yes" or "uh-huh," you must rely heavily on non-verbal cues. Maintain consistent, gentle eye contact, nod warmly, lean forward, and reflect appropriate facial expressions to show empathy and understanding. These visual signals reassure the subject that their story is resonating and encourages them to continue opening up.

Respect Boundaries and Offer Control

Vulnerability requires trust, and trust requires agency. Explicitly tell the subject that they have the right to pause at any time, take a water break, or decline to answer a question that feels too intrusive. If a subject becomes visibly emotional, resist the urge to immediately push for dramatic effect. Check in with them, respect their emotional space, and let them set the pace. When subjects know their boundaries are respected, their guard drops, resulting in the candid and powerful moments that define great documentary storytelling.
