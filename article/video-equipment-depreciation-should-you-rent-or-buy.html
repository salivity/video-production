Video Equipment Depreciation: Should You Rent or Buy?

For a video production company, choosing between purchasing gear and renting it—the classic make-or-buy decision—is fundamentally an analysis of total cost, cash flow, and technological lifespan. Equipment depreciation is the financial mechanism at the center of this choice. Because video technology advances rapidly, depreciation reflects not just an accounting write-off for tax purposes, but the real-world loss of asset value due to wear and obsolescence. Understanding how depreciation affects your tax liabilities, balance sheet, and operating margins is critical to determining whether capital expenditure (CapEx) or operational expenditure (OpEx) makes more financial sense for your business.

The True Cost of Equipment Depreciation

Depreciation represents the gradual reduction in an asset's recorded value over time. In video production, equipment generally falls into two categories of depreciation:

  1. Slow-Depreciating Assets: Items such as tripods, c-stands, prime lenses, and audio microphones often retain functional and market value for five to ten years or more.
  2. Fast-Depreciating Assets: Camera bodies, digital sensors, monitors, and media cards often suffer steep depreciation within two to three years due to rapid sensor innovations, resolution upgrades, and software shifts.

When you purchase gear, you take on this loss of equity. While tax codes often allow production companies to soften the blow through mechanisms like Section 179 deductions or bonus depreciation—which permit writing off the full or substantial purchase price in the first year—the physical asset continues to lose resale value on the secondary market. If a $15,000 camera loses 50% of its market value within 24 months, that $7,500 difference is a direct cost of ownership that must be recovered through client billing.

Renting as an Alternative: Shifting the Depreciation Risk

When a video production company rents equipment, it offloads the burden of asset depreciation entirely onto the rental house.

Rental fees are classified as immediate operating expenses (OpEx), which are fully deductible against current revenue in the tax year they are incurred. This provides several direct advantages:

  • Elimination of Obsolescence Risk: You always have access to the latest cinema cameras without carrying dead stock when market standards advance from 4K to 6K or 8K.
  • Predictable Project Costing: Rental fees can be passed directly to clients as line-item expenses, preserving working capital and matching expenses exactly to revenue events.
  • No Maintenance and Storage Liabilities: Owned equipment requires ongoing insurance, secure storage, and regular servicing, all of which add to the indirect cost of holding a depreciating asset.

Calculating the Breakeven: Utilization Rates vs. Residual Value

To determine whether to rent or buy, calculate the utilization rate against the projected depreciation schedule. The formula requires estimating:

  • The purchase price and financing costs.
  • Expected maintenance, insurance, and storage over the asset’s life.
  • The residual value (expected market resale value) at the end of the asset's useful life.
  • The daily rental rate for equivalent gear.

If the gear will only be used on 10 to 15 days per year, the daily cost of ownership (depreciation + maintenance) almost always exceeds rental costs. If the equipment will be fielded 50 or more days a year, the revenue generated generally outpaces the rate of depreciation, making purchase the more profitable path.

Strategic Allocation: When to Buy vs. When to Rent

A balanced production company often adopts a hybrid model governed by depreciation rates:

  • Buy Low-Depreciation "Evergreen" Gear: Invest in lighting packages, high-end cinema glass, grip equipment, and essential audio tools. These items depreciate slowly, maintain high rental parity, and yield strong returns long after they are fully depreciated on your balance sheet.
  • Rent High-Depreciation Digital Gear: Rent flagship camera bodies, niche specialty lenses (such as high-end anamorphics or extreme telephotos), and complex drone setups. By renting, you insulate your company from rapid market depreciation and guarantee your clients the newest production tools without tying up your balance sheet.
