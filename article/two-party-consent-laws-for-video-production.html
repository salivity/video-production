Two-Party Consent Laws for Video Production

Recording audio during video production in two-party (or all-party) consent jurisdictions requires filmmakers and videographers to obtain permission from every individual whose voice is captured. Failing to secure consent before recording can lead to severe criminal wiretapping charges, civil lawsuits, and the complete legal inadmissibility or forced destruction of the recorded footage. Navigating these jurisdictions requires a clear understanding of what constitutes a conversation, where individuals have a reasonable expectation of privacy, and how to properly document consent across various production environments.

What Is a Two-Party Consent Jurisdiction?

In the United States, audio recording laws are governed at both the federal and state levels. Federal law requires only "one-party consent," meaning a recording is legal as long as one person participating in the conversation consents to it. However, several states—including California, Florida, Massachusetts, Pennsylvania, Illinois, Washington, Maryland, Montana, New Hampshire, and Delaware—enforce "two-party" or "all-party" consent laws. In these states, recording audio of a private conversation without the knowledge and permission of all active participants is illegal.

The Standard of "Reasonable Expectation of Privacy"

The core legal test in two-party consent jurisdictions is whether the speaker had an objectively reasonable expectation of privacy:

  • Private Locations: In private homes, offices, dressing rooms, or closed conference areas, the expectation of privacy is absolute. Explicit consent is legally mandatory for any audio recording.
  • Public Locations: In public parks, streets, and rallies, the expectation of privacy is significantly lower. While recording general ambient sound or public statements is typically permissible, capturing private, quiet discussions between individuals on a long-range directional microphone (shotgun mic) without their knowledge can still constitute an illegal interception of communication.

Legal Requirements to Secure Valid Consent

To satisfy two-party consent laws, video production teams must demonstrate that every recorded individual consented voluntarily and with full knowledge that audio was being captured. Valid consent can be acquired through the following methods:

  1. Written Appearance Releases: The gold standard in video production. A signed talent release explicitly states that the individual consents to both visual and audio recording for commercial or non-commercial distribution.
  2. Recorded Verbal Consent: Asking the participant on camera, while the audio is actively rolling, if they consent to being recorded for the specific project. This creates an unshakeable timestamped record.
  3. Notices and Crowd Releases: When filming in semi-public or privately owned public spaces (such as event halls, restaurants, or conventions), prominently posted entry signage must inform attendees that audio and video recording are taking place. Entering the designated space after passing the clear notice constitutes implied consent under the law in most jurisdictions, though this method does not replace written releases for primary subjects.

Penalties for Non-Compliance

Operating in violation of two-party consent laws exposes production companies, producers, and crew members to significant legal liability:

  • Criminal Charges: Many states classify unauthorized audio recording as a felony or high-grade misdemeanor under state wiretapping and eavesdropping statutes, carrying potential jail time and criminal fines.
  • Civil Lawsuits: Unlawfully recorded individuals can sue for statutory damages, actual damages, punitive damages, and legal fees.
  • Injunctions and Project Invalidation: Courts can issue injunctions prohibiting the distribution, broadcasting, or commercial exploitation of any footage containing unlawfully recorded audio. Additionally, errors and omissions (E&O) insurance providers typically refuse to cover claims arising from intentional or negligent violations of wiretap laws.

Best Practices for Production Crews

To prevent legal complications, production crews should establish a standardized protocol:

  • Treat every production environment in a two-party consent state as requiring all-party permission by default.
  • Ensure boom operators and sound recordists cut audio or pause recording during off-camera breaks, informal crew banter, or private participant discussions.
  • Store all physical and digital release forms in tandem with raw audio files for verification during post-production and distribution clearance.
