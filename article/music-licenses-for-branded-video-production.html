Music Licenses for Branded Video Production

Synchronizing commercial music into branded video production requires clearing two fundamental legal rights: the synchronization license and the master use license. Because every commercial song contains two distinct copyrighted elements—the underlying composition and the specific sound recording—brands must secure permissions from both copyright owners before pairing the music with visual content. Failing to obtain both licenses can result in copyright infringement penalties, costly litigation, and immediate removal of the video from marketing channels.

The Two Mandatory Music Licenses

Every commercial track has two separate copyrights held by different parties. To synchronize music to visuals legally, you must acquire permissions for both:

1. The Synchronization (Sync) License

The sync license grants permission to use the underlying musical composition, which includes the melody, arrangement, and lyrics.

  • Who controls it: The songwriter, composer, or their music publisher.
  • Why it is required: Any time music is paired ("synced") with moving images in a commercial, film, or corporate video, a sync license is legally required. If a song has multiple co-writers represented by different publishing companies, you must secure a sync license from every single publisher involved.

2. The Master Use License

The master use license grants permission to use the specific, original recording of the song performed by a particular artist.

  • Who controls it: The record label or the entity that financed and owns the master recording.
  • Why it is required: While the sync license covers the written song, the master license covers the actual audio files of that performance. Even if you hold a sync license, you cannot play the artist’s specific recording without a master use agreement.

Additional Licensing and Distribution Factors

Beyond the primary sync and master licenses, branded campaigns must account for distribution rights:

  • Public Performance Licenses: When a branded video is broadcast on television, radio, or at live corporate events, a public performance license is required. For television and radio, these are typically handled via Performance Rights Organizations (PROs) like ASCAP, BMI, or SESAC. On digital platforms like YouTube or Meta, platforms maintain blanket agreements, though branded content often requires custom clearance to prevent automated copyright strikes.
  • Scope, Territory, and Term: Music licenses are not one-size-fits-all. License agreements strictly define:
    • Media channels: Organic social media, paid digital ads, broadcast television, or cinema.
    • Territory: Regional, national, or worldwide distribution.
    • Duration (Term): A set timeframe (e.g., 6 months, 1 year) or in perpetuity. Using the music beyond the agreed timeframe constitutes copyright infringement.

The Alternative: Royalty-Free and Commercial Stock Music

Because licensing popular commercial tracks from major record labels and publishers often costs tens to hundreds of thousands of dollars, many brands utilize commercial stock music libraries. These platforms provide pre-cleared sync and master rights under commercial micro-licenses specifically tailored for branded content, significantly reducing both cost and legal complexity.
