Scaling Catering and Craft Services by Crew Size

Budgeting catering and craft services on a multi-week video production requires balancing linear per-person food costs with stepwise labor and operational thresholds. As crew size increases from a lean independent unit to a standard mid-tier commercial or feature crew, meal and snack line items transition from simple grocery runs and restaurant drop-offs to dedicated union labor, food trucks, and commercial infrastructure. Understanding how these line items scale across extended schedules ensures production managers avoid budget overruns, dietary miscalculations, and costly union meal penalties.

The Fundamental Cost Split: Per-Head vs. Operational Base

Catering and craft services are budgeted using two distinct financial models: per-person per-day (PPD) food costs and fixed operational expenses.

  • Per-Person Per-Day (PPD) Costs: Food ingredients, beverages, disposable or compostable serviceware, and dietary-specific substitutions scale linearly. If food costs are budgeted at $35 per person for hot lunch and $15 per person for all-day craft services, a crew of 20 costs $1,000 daily, while a crew of 80 scales to $4,000 daily for ingredients alone.
  • Operational and Labor Costs: Equipment rentals, catering trucks, kit rentals, and labor do not scale linearly. They jump at distinct crew thresholds (step-fixed costs). A single craft service key can comfortably manage 25 to 30 people, but adding another 10 crew members often requires hiring an assistant craft service (crafty) technician to maintain prep, sanitation, and restocking.

Craft Services Scaling Across Crew Tiers

Craft services provides continuous food, coffee, and hydration throughout the working day to sustain crew energy between formal meals.

  • Small Crews (Under 15 People): Typically unstaffed. A production assistant performs wholesale club runs at the start of each week. Budgets average $10 to $15 per head per day, with equipment costs limited to basic coolers and folding tables.
  • Mid-Sized Crews (20 to 50 People): Requires a dedicated Craft Service Key. Labor becomes an explicit line item alongside a standard kit rental fee (commercial coffee makers, displays, pop-up tents). PPD food allocations increase to $15 to $25 to accommodate fresh fruit, hot snack passes (e.g., sliders or soup), and diverse dietary needs (vegan, gluten-free).
  • Large Multi-Week Crews (60+ People): Demands a two-to-three-person craft service team (Key and Assistants) plus dedicated utility vehicles or trailers. Food budgets scale to $25+ PPD, accompanied by higher rental line items for commercial refrigeration, multiple hot-holding units, and increased beverage expenditure.

Catering Scaling: Drop-Offs vs. Mobile Kitchens

Production catering covers dedicated sit-down hot meals, primarily lunch (and second meals for extended shooting days).

  • Drop-Off / Restaurant Orders (10–25 People): Multi-week shoots with micro-crews frequently rely on local restaurants or corporate drop-off catering. Costs average $20 to $30 per person. While labor line items are lower, inefficiency and waste run higher, and menu fatigue becomes an issue over several weeks.
  • Dedicated Production Caterers (30–75 People): The threshold where hiring a specialized film catering company becomes cost-effective. These vendors bring dedicated staff, hot lines, and structured menus. Per-head rates hover around $25 to $40, often accompanied by minimum headcount guarantees (e.g., billing for at least 40 heads regardless of call sheet counts).
  • Full Mobile Kitchens (75+ People): On large multi-week productions, caterers deploy self-contained mobile kitchen trucks. This introduces fixed line items for truck mileage, generator fuel, water disposal, kitchen kit rentals, and minimum staffing rates for chefs, line cooks, and dishwashers. Per-head food costs often drop slightly due to wholesale bulk efficiencies, but overall operational line items scale significantly.

Multi-Week Compounding Factors

Over several weeks, scale introduces secondary cost impacts that must be accounted for in the budget:

  1. Volume Discounts and Bulk Purchasing: Extended runs allow caterers and crafty leads to negotiate bulk rates with local suppliers, lowering average per-unit food costs relative to single-day shoots.
  2. Second Meals and Overtime: As crew size scales, turnaround times and setup complexities increase, heightening the risk of working past the six-hour mark. If a second meal is triggered, catering costs double for that day, or production incurs substantial meal penalty fees under union (IATSE, Teamsters) agreements.
  3. Waste Margins: On small sets, a 10% food buffer represents only one or two extra meals. On a 100-person crew, maintaining that same 10% buffer equates to 10 full meals wasted daily if headcount tracking is imprecise. Tight coordination between the Assistant Director department and the catering team regarding daily call sheets is essential to scale portions accurately day-to-day.
