Chroma Subsampling Explained: 4:2:2 vs 4:2:0

Chroma subsampling is a video compression technique that reduces color resolution to save bandwidth and storage while preserving overall image brightness. This article breaks down the technical differences between 4:2:2 and 4:2:0 subsampling, explaining how the reduction of color information directly influences color fidelity, post-production flexibility, visual effects workflows, and final playback quality in video production.

Understanding the Numbers

The human visual system is far more sensitive to variations in brightness (luminance) than to variations in color (chrominance). Video encoders exploit this trait by encoding brightness (\(Y\)) at full resolution while sampling color (\(Cb\) and \(Cr\)) at lower resolutions.

Chroma subsampling is expressed as a three-part ratio (\(J:a:b\)) based on a conceptual grid of four pixels wide by two pixels tall:

  • \(J\) (Reference width): Always 4, representing the horizontal width of the sample grid.
  • \(a\) (Top row color samples): The number of chrominance samples taken in the top row of four pixels.
  • \(b\) (Bottom row color samples): The number of chrominance samples taken in the bottom row of four pixels.

In a 4:4:4 setup, every single pixel has its own distinct color and brightness value, retaining 100% of color information.

In 4:2:2, color is sampled at half the horizontal resolution while keeping full vertical resolution. This retains 50% of the original color data compared to uncompressed video.

In 4:2:0, color is sampled at half the horizontal resolution and half the vertical resolution. This setup retains only 25% of the original color information.

Direct Impact on Color Fidelity

Color fidelity refers to how accurately and cleanly colors are reproduced relative to the source. The difference between 4:2:2 and 4:2:0 manifests in several distinct ways:

1. Color Grading and Dynamic Range

When shooting video destined for heavy color manipulation, 4:2:2 provides a far denser grid of chromatic data. In 4:2:0 footage, pushing saturation, changing color hues, or isolating specific tones (such as skin tones) frequently reveals color banding, macroblocking, and pixelation. Because 4:2:2 preserves twice as much color data, gradients remain smoother when stretching the dynamic range in post-production.

2. Chroma Keying and Visual Effects

Chroma keying relies on precise color borders to separate a subject from a green or blue screen.

  • 4:2:0: Because color boundaries are averaged across 2x2 pixel blocks, high-contrast borders—such as stray hairs, fine fabric textures, or motion-blurred limbs—become jagged or muddy. This leads to color fringing and difficult keys.
  • 4:2:2: Retaining full vertical color resolution yields clean, sharp edges that key cleanly without requiring extensive edge refinement or garbage matting.

3. High-Contrast Color Transitions and Text

In standard viewing, 4:2:0 is virtually indistinguishable from 4:2:2 in natural scenes. However, sharp, high-contrast color boundaries expose 4:2:0 limitations immediately. Pure red text over a black background or sharp geometric graphics will appear blurry, pixelated, or artifact-heavy in 4:2:0 due to the missing color samples. 4:2:2 preserves edge crispness across these contrasting color zones.

Choosing Between 4:2:2 and 4:2:0

  • Capture in 4:2:2 whenever footage requires color correction, log-profile grading, green-screen compositing, or mastering for broadcast television and cinema distribution.
  • Deliver in 4:2:0 for final consumer distribution. Web streaming platforms (like YouTube and Vimeo), Blu-ray discs, and broadcast standards universally downsample to 4:2:0 to minimize file size and streaming bandwidth without significant perceptual loss to the viewer.
