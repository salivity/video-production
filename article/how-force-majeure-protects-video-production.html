How Force Majeure Protects Video Production

Force majeure contract clauses protect video production companies by legally excusing them from fulfilling contractual obligations when unexpected, unavoidable events derail a shoot. In the fast-paced and unpredictable world of video production—where schedules depend heavily on weather, location access, talent availability, and specialized gear—these provisions ensure that uncontrollable delays do not lead to breach-of-contract lawsuits, excessive financial penalties, or catastrophic legal disputes.

Definition of Qualifying Events

A standard force majeure clause explicitly identifies the specific, unforeseen events that are beyond the reasonable control of the production company. In video production, these typically include:

  • Acts of God: Severe weather (hurricanes, blizzards, floods), earthquakes, or natural disasters that render outdoor locations inaccessible or hazardous.
  • Health Crises: Epidemics, pandemics, or government-mandated lockdowns that halt in-person filming or limit gathering sizes.
  • Labor and Industry Actions: Unanticipated strikes, union picketing (such as SAG-AFTRA or IATSE disputes), or sudden industry-wide work stoppages.
  • Civil and State Disruptions: Wars, civil unrest, or the sudden revocation of government permits required to film in specific public areas.

Suspension of Deadlines Without Default

When an unforeseen event strikes, the force majeure clause acts as a legal shield by suspending production deadlines. Without this clause, failing to deliver a finished cut on the agreed date could expose the production house to breach-of-contract claims from the client. With the clause enacted, performance obligations are paused for the duration of the disruptive event without triggering default status or late-delivery damages.

Allocation of Sunk Costs and Additional Expenses

Video production requires upfront financial commitments, including non-refundable location fees, equipment rentals, and crew day rates. A properly drafted force majeure clause establishes how these intermediate costs are handled during a delay:

  • Crew and Gear Holds: It clarifies whether the client or the production house bears the cost of extended equipment rentals or crew cancellation fees.
  • Retainers and Deposits: It prevents clients from automatically demanding a full refund of upfront deposits when work is temporarily suspended, ensuring the company can cover committed out-of-pocket costs.

Mutual Rights of Termination

If a disruptive event persists indefinitely, neither party should be trapped in an unfulfillable agreement. Force majeure clauses typically include an "off-ramp" specifying that if a delay lasts beyond a set period (commonly 30 to 60 days), either the client or the production company may terminate the contract entirely. Upon termination, the clause dictates that the production company is compensated for all work completed up to the date of disruption, protecting their baseline revenue.

Mitigation and Notice Requirements

To maintain legal protection under force majeure, production companies are typically required to:

  1. Provide Prompt Written Notice: Formally notify the client as soon as the unforeseen event occurs, explaining its direct impact on the production schedule.
  2. Mitigate Damages: Demonstrate reasonable efforts to minimize the delay, such as rescheduling indoor shoots, re-routing logistics, or securing alternative locations if viable.

By defining uncontrollable disruptions and outlining clear financial and operational protocols, force majeure clauses safeguard production companies against financial devastation when real-world circumstances halt production.
