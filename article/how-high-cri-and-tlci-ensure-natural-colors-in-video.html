How High CRI and TLCI Ensure Natural Colors in Video

Achieving realistic colors in modern video production relies heavily on the quality of light sources, evaluated primarily through Color Rendering Index (CRI) and Television Lighting Consistency Index (TLCI). This article breaks down what CRI and TLCI ratings measure, why continuous light spectrums are essential for camera sensors, and how fixtures scoring 95 or higher guarantee authentic skin tones while streamlining the post-production color-grading process.

Understanding CRI and TLCI

CRI measures a light source's ability to reveal colors realistically compared to natural daylight or a standard reference source. Calculated on a scale from 0 to 100, standard CRI tests eight pastel color samples (R1–R8), while extended CRI adds saturated colors, notably R9 (deep red). While CRI evaluates light as perceived by the human eye, it does not fully account for how digital cameras process light.

TLCI solves this limitation by measuring light through the perspective of a standard camera sensor and display system. Using a 24-color chart similar to the standard Macbeth chart, TLCI generates a score from 0 to 100. A TLCI score between 85 and 100 indicates that color errors are small enough that a colorist will not need to make complex secondary corrections.

Eliminating Spectral Gaps

Traditional incandescent lights emit a continuous, smooth spectrum, but modern energy-efficient LED fixtures generate light by blending phosphors with blue or ultraviolet diodes. Poorly engineered LEDs often produce discontinuous spectrums with harsh peaks in the blue or green wavelengths and significant dips in the red spectrum.

High-CRI and high-TLCI lights feature balanced, continuous Spectral Power Distributions (SPD). By providing uniform coverage across the visible spectrum, these fixtures ensure that no critical wavelengths are missing. If a light source fails to emit a specific wavelength, the physical object cannot reflect it, making it impossible for the camera sensor to record that color data accurately.

Accurately Rendering Human Skin Tones

Human skin contains diverse layers of pigments, hemoglobin, and melanin, requiring a full spectrum of light—especially deep red—to appear vibrant and healthy. Fixtures with low CRI or low R9 values cause skin to look plasticky, pale, or sickly green.

When lighting has an extended CRI (including R9) and a TLCI score of 95 or higher, the red and amber spectrums penetrate and reflect naturally from the skin. The camera sensor captures genuine skin warmth, tonal depth, and realistic contrast between highlights and shadows without unnatural color casts.

Consistency Across Multiple Camera Systems

Every camera brand designs its sensors and color science differently, utilizing unique color filter arrays (CFAs). Under discontinuous, low-rated lighting, two different cameras on the same set may interpret missing wavelengths in drastically different ways, creating severe tint mismatches between angles.

High-TLCI lights standardise the environment. Because the light output matches standard broadcast response models, different camera bodies capture consistent baseline color data, significantly reducing variation across multi-camera shoots.

Reducing Post-Production Costs and Artifacts

Fixing color deficiencies caused by low-quality lighting in post-production is time-consuming and often destructive to footage. Pushing and pulling color wheels to correct missing spectrums introduces digital noise, destroys gradient roll-offs, and breaks compressed video codecs.

Lights with CRI and TLCI ratings above 95 deliver accurate rendition directly in-camera. This reduces the time spent on corrective grading, allowing colorists to focus on creative styling rather than repair work.
