How to Use False Color for Video Exposure

False color is an essential exposure monitoring tool that translates the brightness values of an image into a distinct spectrum of colors. This article explains the fundamentals of false color, breaks down how it reads luminance via IRE scales, and demonstrates how cinematographers rely on it on set to achieve consistent skin tones, protect highlight and shadow details, and ensure precise exposure across varied lighting environments.

What Is False Color?

False color is an exposure assist tool built into professional cinema cameras and production monitors. Instead of showing the true colors of a scene, it replaces visual information with an artificial color overlay representing luminance values.

The tool measures brightness using IRE (Institute of Radio Engineers) units, ranging from 0 (absolute black) to 100 (pure white), and sometimes beyond 100 to indicate digital sensor clipping. Each specific IRE range corresponds to a predetermined color, turning the monitor into an intuitive, real-time exposure heat map.

The Standard False Color Spectrum

While manufacturers such as ARRI, RED, and SmallHD have minor variations in their color mapping, most follow an industry-standard hierarchy:

  • Purple / Dark Blue (0–10 IRE): Deep shadows, underexposure, and clipped blacks with no recoverable data.
  • Light Blue (10–25 IRE): Shadow details just above pure black.
  • Green (38–45 IRE): 18% neutral gray, which serves as the benchmark for standard middle-gray exposure in digital sensors.
  • Pink / Light Green (45–70 IRE): Typical optimal ranges for human skin tones. Pink generally represents lighter complexions (~55–60 IRE), while darker complexions often fall in the light green or mid-tone bands (~40–50 IRE).
  • Yellow / Orange (80–99 IRE): Bright highlights, such as bright skies, hot practical lights, or reflective surfaces approaching the sensor's limit.
  • Red (100+ IRE): Blown-out highlights, total sensor saturation, and unrecoverable clipped data.

How Cinematographers Use False Color

1. Nailing Consistent Skin Tones

Human skin is often the most critical exposure element in a scene. Visual estimation through a monitor can be deceptive, particularly when viewing flat Log profiles or working outdoors under changing ambient light. Cinematographers use false color to verify that an actor's face consistently hits the targeted IRE color value across wide shots, close-ups, and varying lighting setups.

2. Protecting Dynamic Range

False color eliminates the guesswork around clipping. Cinematographers can instantly spot whether a practical light or a window is blowing out by looking for red patches, or verify whether shadow areas are dropping into crushed purple zones. This enables rapid adjustments to aperture, ND filters, or lighting ratios before rolling.

3. Exposure Independence from Display LUTs

Look-Up Tables (LUTs) applied to production monitors can alter perceived exposure and contrast. False color can be set to read the raw sensor signal directly beneath the LUT. This ensures exposure decisions are made based on the actual digital negative recorded to the media rather than the aesthetic look applied to the monitor.

4. Shot-to-Shot Lighting Matching

When filming multi-camera setups or returning to a set for reshoots, false color allows camera operators and DPs to reproduce the exact lighting ratios of previous takes. By memorizing or documenting the color distribution of key elements, crews can match lighting setups precisely, reducing corrective color grading in post-production.
