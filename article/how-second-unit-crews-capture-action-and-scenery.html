How Second Unit Crews Capture Action and Scenery

Second unit crews play a critical role in high-level video production by operating concurrently with the main production team to film essential cutaways, landscape vistas, complex stunts, and tight action inserts. By working in tandem with the primary unit, these specialized teams drastically reduce shooting schedules and production costs while ensuring the film has all necessary visual assets for editing. This guide breaks down the organizational, technical, and logistical workflows that allow a second unit to capture high-quality establishing shots and insert coverage that seamlessly match the main production.

Tactical Communication and Pre-Visualization

To maintain visual and narrative continuity, second unit operations depend on meticulous pre-production alignment with the main unit director and director of photography (DP).

  • Storyboards and Shot Lists: The second unit director works from dedicated shot lists extracted from the master shooting script. Detailed storyboards and previs (pre-visualization) 3D animatics dictate the exact framing, lens choice, and camera movement required.
  • Shared Dailies and Live Feeds: Modern productions frequently use cloud-based video feeds and digital dailies. This allows the primary director to review footage captured by the second unit on the same day—or even in real time—and request adjustments immediately.
  • Camera and Lighting Logs: The first unit DP provides precise technical notes, including color temperature, T-stop, filtration, focal length, and lighting setups, guaranteeing that cutaways match the master scenes.

Capturing Establishing Scenery and Plates

Establishing shots set the tone, geography, and scale of a scene. Because these shots often do not require the principal actors, the second unit handles them independently.

  • Chasing Natural Light: Second units are frequently dispatched during optimal natural lighting windows—such as golden hour or blue hour—to capture skyline vistas, remote landscapes, and dynamic weather patterns without holding up stage-bound dialogue scenes.
  • Drone and Aerial Cinematography: Specialized aerial crews fly drones or operate helicopter-mounted gimbal systems (such as Shotover or Cineflex rigs) to film large-scale geographic overviews and sweeping tracking shots.
  • VFX Plates: When principal scenes take place against green screens or inside LED volume stages, the second unit is tasked with capturing the real-world background plates. They shoot multi-camera arrays and high-dynamic-range panoramic images (HDRI) to capture the environment's exact reflections and lighting.

Filming Action Inserts Concurrently

Action inserts consist of rapid, detailed cutaways—such as a hand shifting gears in a car chase, a foot hitting a brake pedal, or a weapon being drawn. Capturing these shots demands speed, high-speed photography, and specialized safety protocols.

  • Stunt Doubles and Specialized Rigs: While the first unit shoots close-ups with lead actors, the second unit utilizes stunt doubles and precision drivers to shoot high-risk stunts, vehicular chases, and pyrotechnics on alternate locations or adjacent stages.
  • Dedicated Insert Stages: A second unit often sets up a small "splinter" stage near the main production. Here, they utilize macro lenses, turntable rigs, and high-frame-rate cameras to shoot non-actor-dependent details, like text messages on phones, ticking clocks, or intricate props.
  • High-Speed and Crash Cams: Action inserts frequently require slow-motion footage to emphasize impact. The second unit deploys specialized cinema cameras (such as Phantom cameras) or compact "crash cams" placed directly in the path of debris, vehicles, or simulated explosions.

Ensuring Seamless Visual Consistency

Footage from the second unit must intercut with the first unit’s footage invisibly. If the audience can tell a different crew filmed a shot, the illusion fails.

  • Matching Glass and Sensors: The second unit typically uses the exact same camera bodies and matched sets of cinema lenses as the main unit to ensure identical depth of field, flare characteristics, and color science.
  • Matching LUTs (Look-Up Tables): Both units monitor their footage using the project's standard show LUT. This ensures exposure and contrast decisions align with the final intended color grade.
  • Recreating Lighting Conditions: For close inserts involving characters' hands or bodies, the second unit's gaffer mirrors the precise key-to-fill ratios, diffusion, and shadow directions established by the main unit, even if the insert is shot weeks later on an entirely different soundstage.
