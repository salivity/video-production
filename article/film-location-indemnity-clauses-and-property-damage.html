Film Location Indemnity Clauses and Property Damage

Location indemnity clauses are essential legal provisions within film location agreements that define who pays if a property is damaged during a shoot. These clauses explicitly allocate financial and legal liability between the production company and the property owner. By establishing "hold harmless" terms, outlining insurance requirements, and defining the scope of responsibility, indemnity clauses protect property owners from out-of-pocket repair costs while ensuring filmmakers understand the exact operational boundaries and liabilities of using a private or commercial space.

What Is a Location Indemnity Clause?

An indemnity clause is a contractual promise where one party agrees to compensate the other for specified loss or damage. In video and film production agreements, the production company typically acts as the indemnitor, agreeing to defend, indemnify, and hold harmless the property owner (the indemnitee) from claims, lawsuits, damages, and expenses arising out of the production's activities on the premises.

How Responsibility Is Typically Assigned

In standard video production contracts, liability for property damage follows specific boundaries:

  • Cast and Crew Actions: The production company is liable for any physical damage caused by cast members, crew, background actors, or equipment. This includes floor scratches from heavy lighting stands, wall gouges from moving gear, or electrical surges caused by production power tie-ins.
  • Property Alterations: If a shoot requires painting, removing doors, or altering landscaping, the indemnity clause binds the production company to restore the property to its original condition or pay for professional restoration.
  • Third-Party Claims: If a production activity causes damage that spills over to neighboring properties (e.g., water leaks or generator fires), the indemnification clause shields the property owner from lawsuits initiated by those third parties.

Standard Exceptions to Production Liability

Indemnity clauses are rarely absolute. Well-drafted agreements include carve-outs where the production company is not held liable:

  • Owner Negligence or Misconduct: The production company will not cover damage resulting from the property owner’s own gross negligence, willful misconduct, or structural failures inherent to the building (such as a pre-existing roof leak that collapses during a shoot).
  • Pre-existing Conditions: Wear and tear or existing defects present before the crew arrives remain the owner’s financial responsibility.
  • Permitted Wear and Tear: Some contracts include clauses acknowledging that light, unavoidable wear and tear (like standard foot traffic on carpeting) does not constitute compensable damage.

The Connection Between Indemnity and Insurance

An indemnity clause is only as strong as the production company’s ability to pay. Therefore, location agreements pair indemnity clauses directly with insurance mandates:

  • Third-Party Property Damage (TPPD) Coverage: This insurance directly backs the indemnity obligation, funding repairs if the crew damages the location.
  • Additional Insured Endorsements: The production company is usually required to name the property owner as an "Additional Insured" on their Commercial General Liability (CGL) policy. This gives the owner direct access to the insurer's claims process if damage occurs.
  • Certificate of Insurance (COI): The owner verifies this protection by requiring a COI before allowing any equipment or personnel on the premises.

Preventing Disputes: Walkthroughs and Documentation

Because indemnity clauses hinge on proving causation, the practical enforcement of these clauses relies on thorough documentation:

  1. Pre-Production Walkthrough: Both parties inspect the space before load-in, creating a signed condition report supported by date-stamped photos and videos.
  2. Post-Production Walkthrough: Immediately following wrap and load-out, a second walkthrough determines if any new damage occurred.
  3. Notification Windows: Agreements usually establish a strict timeframe (e.g., 48 hours to 5 business days) within which the owner must notify the production company of any hidden damage discovered after wrap.
