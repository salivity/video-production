Trademark Clearance in Video: Why Logos Get Blurred

Trademark clearance is the legal review process video producers use to identify, evaluate, and secure permissions for brand names, logos, and recognizable products appearing in their footage. In broadcast television and film, production teams routinely clear or blur third-party logos to avoid trademark infringement lawsuits, protect network advertising revenue, satisfy production insurance requirements, and prevent implied endorsements. Understanding how trademark clearance works and why blurring is necessary is essential for anyone producing broadcast-quality video.

What is Trademark Clearance?

Trademark clearance is the process of vetting all visible brand assets within a production to ensure the project does not infringe on intellectual property rights. Before a show airs or streams, legal specialists review every frame to catalog logos, slogans, brand names, and distinct product designs—such as a soda can, a laptop emblem, or a designer handbag. The clearance team then determines whether a specific logo requires a formal licensing agreement, falls under fair use, or must be removed or obscured before broadcast.

Why Logos Must Be Cleared or Blurred

Broadcast networks, streaming platforms, and film studios follow strict clearance guidelines for several distinct legal, financial, and operational reasons:

1. Avoiding False Endorsement and Dilution Claims

Under trademark law, a trademark owner has the exclusive right to use their mark in commerce to signify the source of goods or services. If a logo is prominently featured in a broadcast, the brand owner might argue that viewers will mistakenly believe the brand sponsored, produced, or endorsed the show. Additionally, if a brand is depicted in a negative, offensive, or defamatory context—such as a villain wearing a branded jacket or a defective product causing harm—the owner could file a trademark tarnishment or dilution lawsuit.

2. Satisfying Errors and Omissions (E&O) Insurance

Nearly every commercial broadcast production requires Errors and Omissions (E&O) insurance to protect the production company and distributors against copyright infringement, trademark violations, and defamation claims. Insurance underwriters will not issue a policy without a comprehensive clearance log showing that all intellectual property has been licensed, cleared, or removed. Without E&O insurance, major networks and streaming platforms will refuse to distribute the project.

3. Protecting Advertising and Sponsorship Revenues

Broadcast networks rely heavily on paid advertising. If a television show gives free, unprompted screen time to a major brand, it devalues the network's commercial airtime. Furthermore, displaying an uncleared logo can create direct conflicts with official sponsors. For example, if an automaker spends millions to be the exclusive automotive sponsor of a broadcast, the network cannot air scenes featuring rival car logos prominently without risking the loss of that sponsor.

4. Meeting Strict Delivery Requirements

Distributors, networks, and global streaming platforms provide independent filmmakers and production companies with strict technical and legal delivery specifications. These deliverables almost always state that footage must be free of unauthorized third-party intellectual property. Blurring an uncleared logo guarantees that the program meets these contractual requirements, preventing costly distribution delays.

How Productions Handle Logos

When a production cannot or chooses not to license a logo, teams generally employ one of three methods to resolve the issue:

  • Production Clearance ("Greeking"): Art departments routinely conceal or alter real logos on set using tape, custom labels, or generic packaging—a practice historically referred to as "greeking."
  • Fictional Props: Productions often create entirely fictional brands (such as fictional beer or cereal brands) to dress sets and avoid legal complications altogether.
  • Post-Production Blurring and Replacement: If an unauthorized logo accidentally appears during a shoot, editors use tracking masks to blur, pixelate, or digitally paint out the emblem before the final cut is submitted for broadcast.
