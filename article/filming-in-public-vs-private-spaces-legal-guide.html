Filming in Public vs Private Spaces: Legal Guide

Commercial video production requires navigating strict legal frameworks that vary significantly depending on whether a shoot takes place on public or private property. While public property provides greater baseline access to visual capture, it typically involves stringent municipal permitting, insurance mandates, and public safety regulations. Conversely, filming on private property hinges entirely on explicit owner consent, contractual location releases, and property rights. Understanding these distinctions is critical for commercial producers to avoid costly shutdowns, trespassing charges, right-of-publicity claims, and copyright infringement.

Access Rights and Trespassing

The legal baseline for accessing a location differs fundamentally between public and private venues:

  • Public Spaces: Public property encompasses government-owned spaces such as streets, sidewalks, and municipal parks. While individual citizens and journalists enjoy broad First Amendment protections to record in these areas, commercial video production does not receive the same leniency. Using public infrastructure for commercial gain is treated as an extraordinary use of public resources, giving local authorities the right to regulate the time, place, and manner of production.
  • Private Spaces: Private property includes residential homes, corporate offices, retail centers, and private land. Film crews have no automatic right to enter, set up gear, or record on private property. Filming without explicit authorization constitutes civil trespass and, in some jurisdictions, criminal trespass. Revocation of consent can occur at any time unless a binding contract states otherwise.

Permitting and Insurance Mandates

Permitting requirements dictate how a production can legally operate on-site:

  • Public Permitting: Commercial shoots on public property almost universally require a film permit issued by the local city, county, or state film office. This process involves submitting detailed production schedules, equipment lists, and site maps. Additionally, municipalities mandate proof of Comprehensive General Liability (CGL) insurance, usually naming the city as an "additional insured," along with permits for street closures, noise variances, and pyrotechnics.
  • Private Authorization: Private locations typically do not require municipal film permits, provided the production remains completely enclosed within the property boundaries. However, if cast, crew, production vehicles, or lighting rigs spill over onto adjacent public sidewalks or streets, city permits become necessary. Production on private property relies on a private contract known as a Location Agreement rather than a government permit.

Reasonable Expectation of Privacy and Talent Releases

Privacy torts and the right of publicity apply differently depending on the setting:

  • In Public: Individuals in public spaces generally have a low "reasonable expectation of privacy." While incidental capture of pedestrians is common, using an identifiable individual's image or likeness in a commercial video—such as an advertisement or brand promotion—without a signed talent release violates their right of publicity. Crowd release notices may suffice for background atmospheres in specific contexts, but individual releases remain the legal standard for commercial exploitation.
  • In Private: Individuals within private spaces maintain a high expectation of privacy. Secretly recording people or capturing footage of individuals inside private dwellings without consent violates state privacy laws and wiretapping statutes. For private shoots, signed talent releases are mandatory for everyone appearing on camera, and location releases must explicitly grant permission to depict the property itself.

Intellectual Property and Property Releases

Securing the legal right to distribute captured imagery requires distinct releases:

  • Property Releases: A Location Agreement for private property must include a comprehensive property release. This clause grants the producer irrevocable rights to photograph the interior and exterior of the property and exploit that footage globally across all media in perpetuity. Without this, property owners can block the distribution of the commercial project.
  • Architectural and Trademark Concerns: On public property, the visual capture of publicly visible buildings is generally protected under copyright law (such as Section 120(a) of the U.S. Copyright Act for architectural works). On private property, however, identifiable logos, branded interior designs, proprietary decor, and fine art pieces displayed within the premises can trigger trademark and copyright infringement claims if not cleared or obscured prior to production.
