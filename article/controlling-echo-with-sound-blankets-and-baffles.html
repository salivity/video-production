Controlling Echo with Sound Blankets and Baffles

In non-treated video production spaces, sound blankets and acoustic baffling control unwanted audio reflections by absorbing airborne sound energy and preventing it from bouncing off hard architectural boundaries. This guide explains the physics behind acoustic absorption in temporary shooting locations, how moving blankets and hanging baffles disrupt flutter echo and comb filtering, and the most effective placement strategies to ensure broadcast-quality dialogue recording on set.

The Acoustic Problem in Non-Treated Spaces

Most non-dedicated filming locations—such as corporate offices, domestic living rooms, warehouses, and classrooms—feature parallel, acoustically reflective surfaces like drywall, glass windows, polished concrete, or hardwood floors. When a subject speaks, their voice radiates omnidirectionally. The microphone captures the direct sound first, followed milliseconds later by reflected sound waves bouncing off these hard surfaces.

This interaction causes three major issues:

  • Flutter Echo: High-frequency sound trapped between parallel walls, creating a rapid, ringing tail.
  • Comb Filtering: Phase cancellation caused when the direct sound and reflected sound combine at slightly different arrival times, resulting in hollow, thin-sounding dialogue.
  • Elevated Reverberation Time (RT60): Prolonged decay of acoustic energy that smears consonants and reduces speech intelligibility.

How Sound Blankets Work

Sound blankets (often called production or furniture blankets) are dense, quilted textiles filled with recycled cotton, polyester, or mineral fibers. They control reflections through viscous friction:

  • Porosity and Friction: When sound waves enter the open-cell, fibrous structure of the blanket, the vibrating air particles collide with the fibers. This mechanical resistance converts acoustic energy into microscopic amounts of heat, eliminating the bounce.
  • Mid-to-High Frequency Attenuation: Because of their thickness (typically 0.5 to 1 inch), standard sound blankets primarily attenuate frequencies above 500 Hz. This frequency band contains speech sibilance, harsh room slap, and the high-frequency reflections that degrade dialogue clarity.
  • The Air Gap Advantage: Sound blankets operate with increased efficiency when hung several inches away from a wall rather than flat against it. This space forces the sound wave to pass through the blanket twice—once on its way to the hard wall, and once more upon reflection—effectively doubling absorption, especially in the lower midrange.

How Acoustic Baffling Works

Acoustic baffles are thicker, often semi-rigid panels typically constructed from dense fiberglass, rockwool, or open-cell acoustic foam wrapped in acoustically transparent fabric. While blankets handle high frequencies, baffles target a wider, deeper frequency spectrum:

  • Two-Sided Absorption: Unlike panels mounted flush to a wall, baffles are suspended vertically or horizontally in open space. Both sides of the baffle are exposed to the room, yielding twice the effective surface area for sound absorption.
  • Low-Mid Frequency Control: Baffles typically measure 2 to 4 inches in thickness and feature higher material density than blankets. This mass allows them to absorb lower frequencies (down to 250 Hz and below), counteracting the "boomy" room resonance often excited by deeper voices.
  • Breaking Standing Waves: Suspended at strategic intervals, baffles break up large volumes of open air, disrupting standing waves before they can build between opposing parallel boundaries.

Deployment Strategies in Field Production

Video crews must place absorption tools outside the camera's field of view while maximizing their acoustic effect:

  • Out-of-Frame Perimeters: Sound blankets can be clamped to C-stands fitted with boom arms or curtain rigs to create a temporary "acoustic tent" around the talent. Placing blankets behind the talent prevents vocal energy from hitting the wall behind them, while placing blankets behind the camera prevents bounce-back toward the microphone.
  • Overhead Reflection Taming: In rooms with low ceilings, hard floor-to-ceiling reflections often compromise boom microphone performance. Hanging lightweight baffles or sound blankets horizontally on grip stands directly above the frame line dampens these reflections near the microphone capsule.
  • Floor Dampening: If the floor is concrete, tile, or hardwood and cannot be entirely framed out, laying sound blankets directly on the floor just outside the frame eliminates the immediate floor-bounce reflection.

By using sound blankets to scatter and absorb high-frequency slapback and employing acoustic baffling to tame midrange resonance and vertical reflections, production teams can dramatically increase the direct-to-reverberant sound ratio, ensuring clean, usable dialogue regardless of the shooting environment.
