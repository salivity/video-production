Managing Live Audio Feedback in Cavernous Venues

Managing acoustic feedback in large, cavernous venues during a live video production requires a multi-layered strategy that combines precise speaker placement, surgical equalization, microphone discipline, and dedicated mix isolation. Cavernous spaces like arenas, cathedrals, and convention centers create long reverberation times and severe room resonance, making audio systems prone to feedback loops. This article covers the essential techniques audio engineers use to maintain high gain-before-feedback while delivering pristine, intelligible sound for both the in-house audience and the live video broadcast.

1. Directional PA Design and Acoustic Coverage

In cavernous spaces, acoustic energy bouncing off reflective surfaces—such as concrete floors, glass, and high ceilings—drastically increases the risk of feedback. Engineers mitigate this by using directional line arrays and steered column speakers aimed strictly at audience seating rather than reflective boundaries. By utilizing delay towers distributed throughout the hall, engineers can run the main front-of-house (FOH) speakers at lower volumes while maintaining even coverage and high speech intelligibility across the entire venue.

2. Eliminating Stage Monitors with In-Ear Systems

Floor monitor wedges are one of the primary sources of acoustic feedback on large stages. In cavernous spaces, wedge sound reflects off the back wall and re-enters vocal microphones. Engineers heavily rely on In-Ear Monitor (IEM) systems for performers and presenters. Removing open stage wedges eliminates a significant feedback vector, clears up stage volume, and prevents unwanted audio bleed into open broadcast microphones.

3. Microphone Selection and Placement

Engineers select tight directional microphones—primarily cardioid or supercardioid models—to reject off-axis sound coming from the room and PA. Key microphone management strategies include:

  • Close Miking: Keeping the capsule as close to the sound source as possible maximizes the signal-to-noise ratio and provides higher gain-before-feedback.
  • Minimizing Open Mics: Leaving unneeded microphones open compounds room bleed and lowers overall feedback thresholds. Engineers use digital gates, manual mutes, or automated mixing algorithms (like Dan Dugan automixers for multi-mic panel setups) to deactivate inactive microphones instantly.

4. Ringing Out the Room with Parametric EQ

Before the production begins, engineers "ring out" the system. They gradually push the overall gain until specific resonant frequencies begin to ring, then apply narrow notch filters (using parametric or dynamic EQs) to cut those exact problem frequencies. In reverberant environments, engineers target room modes—standing waves generated by the physical dimensions of the hall—to ensure the PA operates smoothly without sudden spikes in resonant energy.

5. Splitting the FOH and Broadcast Audio Feeds

In a live video production, the audio requirements for the room and the broadcast are fundamentally different. Engineers split the microphone signals at the stage rack to feed two separate consoles: one for the live venue (FOH) and one for the video broadcast.

  • The FOH engineer shapes the audio specifically to avoid room resonance and feedback over the house PA.
  • The broadcast engineer builds a balanced, full-frequency mix for television or streaming without the heavy EQ cuts needed for the room, ensuring that live room feedback never bleeds into the primary video stream. Audience reaction microphones are strictly routed to the broadcast mix and entirely kept out of the in-house PA to prevent massive feedback loops.
