What Is an Over-the-Shoulder Shot in Video?

This article explores the fundamentals of the over-the-shoulder (OTS) shot in video production, defining its visual characteristics and detailing how filmmakers use it to maintain spatial continuity. Readers will learn the mechanics behind this classic framing technique, its role in upholding the 180-degree rule, and how it grounds the audience within a scene's physical geography.

Defining the Over-the-Shoulder Shot

An over-the-shoulder (OTS) shot is a framing technique where the camera captures a subject from behind the shoulder and back of another character. Typically framed as a medium close-up, the composition places the back of one person's head, neck, and shoulder in the foreground—often slightly blurred or out of focus—while the primary subject facing the camera remains in sharp focus. This framing is the standard visual tool for filming two-character dialogues, interviews, and confrontations.

Establishing Spatial Geometry

The primary technical function of the OTS shot is to visually anchor characters in a specific physical environment. Unlike a single close-up, which isolates an actor against a background, an OTS shot explicitly shows two people in the same frame simultaneously. By keeping a portion of the listener visible, the shot constantly reminds the viewer of the physical distance, height difference, and orientation between the two subjects, eliminating ambiguity about where each person is standing or sitting.

Enforcing the 180-Degree Rule

In visual storytelling, the 180-degree rule dictates that an imaginary axis of action connects the two interacting subjects. To maintain consistent left-to-right spatial orientation, the camera must remain on one side of this line.

The over-the-shoulder shot is essential for enforcing this rule:

  • Consistent Eyelines: By filming matching reverse OTS shots from the same side of the axis, the characters' eyelines naturally cross. Character A looks from screen-left to screen-right, while Character B looks from screen-right to screen-left.
  • Seamless Continuity: When an editor cuts back and forth between two matching OTS shots, the audience perceives a fluid, logical conversation without feeling disoriented or thinking the characters have switched positions.

Creating Depth and Immersion

Beyond geographic clarity, the OTS shot enhances spatial relationships by adding a three-dimensional perspective to a flat screen. The inclusion of the foreground shoulder creates visual layers: foreground (the listener), midground (the speaker), and background (the setting). This layering adds depth to the frame, simulating human perception and positioning the viewer inside the conversational space as an active observer rather than a detached onlooker.
