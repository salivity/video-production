Best Memory Card Speed Class for 4K Video

Choosing the correct memory card speed class for 4K video production requires matching your camera's recording bitrate to the card's minimum sustained write speed. Selecting the wrong card can cause dropped frames, buffer stalls, and corrupted footage during shooting. This guide covers the critical criteria you must evaluate—including Video Speed Classes (V-ratings), bit-to-byte conversion, bus interfaces, and sustained versus burst write performance—to ensure flawless 4K video capture.

1. Calculate Your Camera's Bitrate (Mbps to MB/s)

Camera manufacturers measure video recording bitrates in Megabits per second (Mbps), while memory cards measure write speeds in Megabytes per second (MB/s). Because 8 bits equal 1 byte, you must divide your camera’s bitrate by 8 to determine the actual write speed your card requires.

  • 100 Mbps: Requires at least 12.5 MB/s write speed.
  • 200 Mbps: Requires at least 25 MB/s write speed.
  • 400 Mbps: Requires at least 50 MB/s write speed.

Always provide a 20% to 30% safety margin above your camera's maximum recording bitrate to prevent write errors when the scene complexity spikes.

2. Prioritize the Video Speed Class (V-Rating)

For modern 4K video, the Video Speed Class (designated by the letter "V" followed by a number) is the industry-standard metric. The number denotes the guaranteed minimum sustained write speed in MB/s:

  • V30 (30 MB/s sustained): Ideal for standard 4K recording up to 60fps with compressed codecs (Long GOP / IPB) at bitrates up to 240 Mbps.
  • V60 (60 MB/s sustained): Required for high-bitrate 4K, 10-bit 4:2:2 color profiles, and All-Intra (All-I) recording modes up to 480 Mbps.
  • V90 (90 MB/s sustained): Essential for uncompressed RAW 4K, high-frame-rate capture (such as 4K at 120fps), and maximum-bitrate cinema workflows up to 720 Mbps.

Older ratings like Class 10 (10 MB/s) or UHS Speed Class 1 (U1, 10 MB/s) are insufficient for reliable 4K recording. While UHS Speed Class 3 (U3) matches V30 at 30 MB/s, looking for the V30, V60, or V90 logo ensures the card is certified specifically for modern multi-file video streams.

3. Check Bus Interface Compatibility (UHS-I vs. UHS-II)

SD cards are physically divided into UHS-I and UHS-II bus interfaces:

  • UHS-I: Features a single row of pins and has a theoretical maximum transfer limit of 104 MB/s. UHS-I cards max out at the V30 speed rating.
  • UHS-II: Features a second row of pins, allowing speeds up to 312 MB/s. UHS-II is required for V60 and V90 classifications.

Before buying a V60 or V90 card, check your camera’s card slot specifications. While UHS-II cards are backward-compatible with UHS-I slots, they will down-throttle to UHS-I speeds, rendering them unable to maintain V60 or V90 performance.

4. Ignore Advertised "Read" Speeds and Maximum Bursts

The prominent speed number printed on the front of a memory card box (e.g., "Up to 300 MB/s") almost always refers to peak read speed, which only affects how quickly you can offload footage to a computer.

Video recording requires a continuous, unbroken stream of data. A card with a peak write speed of 150 MB/s that drops down to 15 MB/s during extended writes will fail during a 4K shoot. Only base your decision on the official Video Speed Class rating, which legally certifies the card's minimum sustained write speed.

5. Consider Compression Formats and Frame Rates

The exact 4K format you shoot directly impacts the required speed class:

  • Long GOP / H.264 / H.265 (4K 24/30fps): Uses temporal compression, keeping bitrates modest (50–150 Mbps). A V30 card is typically sufficient.
  • All-Intra (All-I) (4K 24/60fps): Compresses each frame individually, dramatically increasing the file size (200–400 Mbps). A V60 card is recommended.
  • Slow Motion / 4K 120fps: quadruples the frame count, demanding massive throughput. A V90 card (or CFexpress/CFast 2.0 depending on the camera) is required.
