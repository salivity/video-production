Active vs. Passive Lens Adapters for Video

When adapting lenses across different camera mounts in video production, the primary decision lies between active and passive lens adapters. This article explores the core differences between these two adapter types, detailing how electronic communication impacts aperture control, autofocus, image stabilization, and overall production workflow, helping filmmakers choose the right tool for their setup.

Understanding Passive Lens Adapters

A passive lens adapter is a purely mechanical spacer. It allows a lens of one mount type to physically lock onto a camera body with a different mount type while maintaining the correct flange focal distance.

Because passive adapters contain no electronic contacts or internal circuitry, there is zero communication between the camera and the lens.

  • Aperture Control: Modern "fly-by-wire" lenses lack physical aperture rings and rely on electronic signals from the camera to adjust the iris. On a passive adapter, these lenses typically remain locked wide open or at their default aperture. Passive adapters work best with vintage or fully manual cinema lenses that feature physical aperture rings.
  • Focusing: Only manual focus is available. Electronic focus rings without hard stops may fail to work entirely depending on the lens design.
  • Stabilization: In-lens optical stabilization (OIS) will not engage. In-body image stabilization (IBIS) can function, but you must manually input the lens's focal length into the camera menu.
  • Cost and Durability: Passive adapters are inexpensive, have no firmware to update, and rarely fail mechanically.

Understanding Active Lens Adapters

An active lens adapter features electronic pins on both the lens mount and the camera mount, connected by an internal microprocessor. This processor translates camera commands into signals the adapted lens can understand, and vice versa.

  • Aperture Control: Active adapters allow the camera body to electronically adjust the lens aperture via dials or touchscreens. This is mandatory for using modern consumer photo lenses that do not have physical iris rings.
  • Autofocus Performance: Active adapters pass autofocus data between the camera and the lens. While continuous video autofocus can sometimes be slower or prone to hunting compared to native glass, high-end adapters frequently support reliable single-point and face-tracking autofocus.
  • Image Stabilization: Active adapters provide power to the lens, enabling lens-based optical stabilization. They also communicate focal length data directly to the camera body, ensuring automatic and accurate IBIS calibration.
  • Metadata Transmission: Active adapters record crucial EXIF data, such as focal length, aperture setting, and lens model, directly into your video files, which is vital for multi-camera color grading and post-production VFX.
  • Power and Firmware: Active adapters draw power from the camera battery and often require firmware updates via a USB port to maintain compatibility with new camera updates and lens releases.

Key Factors for Video Production

  1. Lens Architecture: If your lens kit consists of fully manual cinema lenses or vintage primes, a passive adapter provides a secure, zero-latency mechanical connection. If you use modern Canon EF, Nikon F, or Sony-compatible lenses with electronic irises, an active adapter is necessary to control exposure.
  2. Speed and Reliability on Set: Passive adapters eliminate electronic communication errors, freezing, or slow startup times. Active adapters introduce a layer of software processing that can occasionally cause camera glitches if the adapter's firmware is out of date.
  3. Budget: Passive adapters are significantly cheaper, often costing a fraction of the price of an active adapter, which incorporates precision electronics and licensing rights.

Which Adapter Should You Choose?

Choose a passive adapter if you shoot exclusively with manual lenses, vintage glass, or dedicated cine primes with built-in gear rings for focus and aperture.

Choose an active adapter if you rely on modern still photography lenses requiring electronic aperture control, want to utilize optical image stabilization, or need autofocus capabilities during production.
