Diegetic vs Non-Diegetic Sound in Video Production

Sound design is a critical storytelling tool in video production that shapes audience emotion, establishes setting, and drives narrative momentum. Audio elements are primarily divided into two categories: diegetic and non-diegetic sound. This article defines both sound types, breaks down their core differences, and explains how filmmakers utilize them to craft immersive cinematic experiences.

What Is Diegetic Sound?

Diegetic sound refers to any audio element that originates from within the fictional world of the film or video. If a character on screen can hear the noise, it is diegetic. These sounds are either captured on set during production or recreated in post-production through Foley and automated dialogue replacement (ADR) to match the visual action.

Common examples of diegetic sound include:

  • Dialogue: Spoken conversations between characters.
  • Foley and Sound Effects: Footsteps, doors slamming, rustling clothes, or glass breaking.
  • Ambient Noise: Natural environmental audio such as wind, rain, traffic, or bird calls.
  • Source Music: Music originating from an on-screen device, such as a car radio, a record player, or a live band performing at a venue.

What Is Non-Diegetic Sound?

Non-Diegetic sound refers to any audio element that exists outside the story world. The characters within the scene cannot hear these sounds; they are added purely for the benefit of the audience. Non-diegetic audio is applied during the editing process to provide emotional context, build tension, or guide narrative pacing.

Common examples of non-diegetic sound include:

  • Film Scores and Soundtracks: Orchestral music or background tracks designed to evoke specific emotions from the audience.
  • Voice-Over Narration: An external or omniscient narrator explaining events, context, or thoughts that other characters cannot perceive.
  • Dramatic Sound Effects: Artificial sound enhancements such as dramatic stingers, whooshes, risers, and bass drops used to punctuate transitions or dramatic moments.

Key Differences Between Diegetic and Non-Diegetic Sound

The distinction between these two audio categories hinges on three factors: origin, perception, and narrative function.

Feature Diegetic Sound Non-Diegetic Sound
Source Within the scene's physical environment Added externally in post-production
Character Perception Audible to the characters Inaudible to the characters
Primary Function Ground the scene in realism and spatial awareness Guide the audience's emotional response and tone
Visual Connection Can often be tied directly to an on-screen source Has no physical source within the visual frame

Blurring the Lines: Trans-Diegetic Sound

Filmmakers often blur the boundary between diegetic and non-diegetic elements to create stylistic transitions, known as trans-diegetic sound. A common technique involves a character turning on a car radio (diegetic), with the song gradually swelling in volume and fidelity until it serves as the background soundtrack (non-diegetic) for an ensuing montage. Another variation is internal diegetic sound, where the audience hears a character's internal thoughts, memories, or subjective auditory hallucinations that exist only in that character's mind.

Mastering both diegetic and non-diegetic sound allows video creators to control how viewers interpret on-screen actions, balancing grounded realism with heightened cinematic drama.
