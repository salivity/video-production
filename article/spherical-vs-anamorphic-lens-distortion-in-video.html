Spherical vs Anamorphic Lens Distortion in Video

In video production, the choice between spherical and anamorphic lenses fundamentally shapes the visual texture, geometry, and emotional tone of a project. While spherical lenses aim to project a geometrically true, uniform image onto the camera sensor, anamorphic lenses intentionally squeeze a wider field of view through cylindrical elements, introducing distinct optical aberrations and non-uniform distortions. This article breaks down the primary differences between spherical and anamorphic lens distortion characteristics, focusing on geometric bending, bokeh rendering, flare behavior, and spatial perception.

Geometric Distortion and Line Curvature

Spherical lenses utilize circular optical elements to project an image uniformly across the camera sensor. Geometric distortion in spherical lenses typically manifests as symmetrical barrel or pincushion distortion:

  • Barrel distortion: The center of the frame magnifies slightly more than the edges, causing straight lines near the perimeter to bow outward.
  • Pincushion distortion: Common in telephoto focal lengths, lines bow inward toward the center.
  • In both cases, the distortion is radially symmetrical, meaning lines curve consistently regardless of whether they are oriented horizontally, vertically, or diagonally.

Anamorphic lenses introduce asymmetrical, non-uniform distortion because they compress the horizontal axis of the image while leaving the vertical axis unaltered. As a result:

  • Straight horizontal lines may appear straight, while vertical lines near the edges curve dramatically inward or outward.
  • When the footage is digitally or optically desqueezed in post-production, this optical compression produces a characteristic horizontal curvature often referred to as anamorphic barrel roll.
  • Close-focusing can cause varying squeeze ratios across the frame, historically known as "anamorphic mumps," where objects or faces widen as they approach the camera unless corrected by modern optical designs.

Bokeh and Out-of-Focus Elements

Distortion extends directly to how out-of-focus background elements are rendered:

  • Spherical Bokeh: Out-of-focus highlights remain circular or take on the geometric shape of the lens iris blades (polygonal). Optical vignetting near the edges of wide-aperture spherical lenses can produce slight "cat-eye" shapes, but the rendering remains mostly symmetrical across the frame.
  • Anamorphic Bokeh: Because the image is compressed horizontally by a factor (commonly 1.33x, 1.5x, 1.8x, or 2x) and then stretched back out in post-production, circular highlights turn into distinct vertical ovals. This vertical elongation applies to the entire background blur, creating a brushstroke-like texture that isolates subjects uniquely from their environments.

Flares and Astigmatic Aberrations

The physical construction of each lens type dictates how light scatters when striking internal glass elements:

  • Spherical Flares: Point light sources entering a spherical lens generate circular, radial veiling glares, halos, or polygonal artifacts that radiate uniformly from the light source.
  • Anamorphic Flares: Cylindrical elements force stray light to spread across the horizontal axis, producing prominent horizontal streaks or line flares. Depending on lens coatings, these streaks can range from vivid sci-fi blues to warm, neutral amber tones. Additionally, chromatic aberration often stretches horizontally rather than radially around frame borders.

Field Curvature and Spatial Compression

Field curvature describes the non-flat focal plane created by optical glass:

  • Spherical lenses are largely engineered to achieve a flat focal plane, ensuring sharpness from edge to edge with minimal falloff.
  • Anamorphic lenses inherently possess pronounced field curvature. The focal plane curves inward toward the camera at the edges, resulting in a center-weighted sweet spot of sharpness accompanied by softer, warped corners. This curvature creates an exaggerated sense of separation between the subject and the background, giving two-dimensional video an organic, three-dimensional depth often referred to as the "anamorphic look."
