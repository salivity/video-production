Fair Use of Archival News Footage in Video Production

Historical video productions frequently rely on archival news footage to establish context, illustrate past events, and anchor narratives in factual reality. Under United States copyright law, the fair use doctrine allows creators to incorporate copyrighted material into their work without permission or payment under specific legal conditions. Applying fair use to historical documentaries and educational video essays requires evaluating four statutory factors: the purpose of the use, the nature of the original work, the amount used, and the effect on the potential market for the original material.

1. Transformative Purpose and Historical Analysis

The primary inquiry in any fair use analysis is whether the secondary use is "transformative." In historical video production, merely using news footage as decorative b-roll or to visually represent an event typically fails the transformation test. Instead, the footage must serve a critical, analytical, or narrative function. If the video uses the broadcast to critique media coverage, analyze how a narrative was presented to the public, or support a specific historical thesis, courts are substantially more likely to deem the use transformative. The footage should be the subject of discussion or directly necessary to prove a point, rather than just serving as convenient wallpaper.

2. The Factual Nature of News Broadcasts

The second factor examines the nature of the copyrighted work. Archival news broadcasts are primarily factual, documentary, and informational, rather than purely creative or fictional. Copyright law grants thinner protection to factual works than to expressive works like feature films or music. Because historical events themselves cannot be copyrighted, using factual broadcasts generally tilts this factor in favor of the filmmaker, provided the material extracted is factual documentation rather than proprietary, artistic staging.

3. Amount and Substantiality of the Excerpt

Producers must use only as much archival footage as is strictly necessary to achieve their specific historical or educational objective. Using an entire twenty-minute news broadcast when a five-second clip establishes the point weakens a fair use claim. Courts evaluate both the quantitative amount (the duration of the clip) and the qualitative value (whether the producer took the "heart" of the work). To remain protected, historical video creators should trim clips down to the precise frames required to illustrate their commentary or historical argument.

4. Market Effect and Licensing Markets

The fourth factor assesses whether the secondary use harms the current or potential market value of the original work. While commercial news archives—such as AP, Reuters, or network licensing desks—actively sell licenses for archival clips, the mere existence of a licensing market does not automatically negate fair use. If the historical video does not act as a direct market substitute for the original broadcast (meaning viewers would not watch the historical video instead of licensing the news footage for its original purpose), this factor often favors fair use. However, using long, unedited segments that could displace the market for archival licensing presents serious legal risk.

Practical Guidelines for Historical Creators

To strengthen a fair use defense when utilizing archival news footage:

  • Directly connect the footage to voiceover commentary, critical analysis, or on-screen discussion.
  • Avoid using news footage purely to avoid the expense of filming original visuals.
  • Keep clips as brief as possible while retaining contextual integrity.
  • Verify if the footage resides in the public domain, such as pre-1929 material or uncopyrighted broadcasts produced by federal government agencies, which can be used freely without fair use analysis.
