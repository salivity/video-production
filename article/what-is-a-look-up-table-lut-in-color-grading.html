What Is a Look Up Table (LUT) in Color Grading?

A Look Up Table (LUT) is a mathematical blueprint used in video production to transform color and contrast values from an input image to a specific desired output. This article covers what a LUT is, the primary types used in post-production, and how editors and colorists integrate them into the color grading workflow to establish baseline color spaces and creative film looks.

What Is a Look Up Table?

In digital video, a Look Up Table is a file containing an index of numbers that alters the color values of footage. Think of it as a complex color-remapping matrix: when an editing software reads a pixel’s original red, green, and blue (RGB) values, the LUT references its internal table and outputs a predetermined new set of RGB values.

LUTs exist primarily in two formats:

  • 1D LUTs: Adjust each color channel (R, G, and B) independently along a single axis. They are suitable for simple contrast and brightness adjustments, but cannot handle complex cross-channel hue shifts.
  • 3D LUTs: Map color on a three-dimensional grid (a color cube). Because they alter hue, saturation, and brightness simultaneously across all channels, 3D LUTs are the standard for high-end video grading and film emulation.

Categories of LUTs in Video Production

In practical workflows, LUTs serve two distinct functions:

  1. Technical (Conversion) LUTs: Digital cinema cameras often record in flat, desaturated profiles known as "Log" (such as Canon Log, Sony S-Log, or ARRI LogC) to preserve maximum dynamic range. Technical LUTs convert this flat data into standard viewing color spaces, such as Rec.709 for standard monitors or Rec.2020 for HDR displays.
  2. Creative LUTs: Once footage is in a standard color space, creative LUTs are applied to introduce a specific artistic tone, mood, or film stock emulation (such as the popular teal-and-orange aesthetic or vintage 35mm print looks).

How to Apply a LUT During Color Grading

Using a LUT correctly requires a structured node-based or layer-based color pipeline (such as in DaVinci Resolve, Premiere Pro, or Final Cut Pro). Applying a LUT incorrectly can compress dynamic range or cause digital image clipping.

1. Normalize the Footage (Technical LUT)

Begin by applying a conversion LUT to translate your flat Log profile to a standard working color space (like Rec.709). This provides a visually accurate representation of contrast and color on your reference monitor.

2. Perform Primary Corrections Before the LUT

Color adjustments should be made upstream of the conversion or creative LUT. Place nodes or adjustment layers before the LUT to balance:

  • Exposure: Adjust lift, gamma, and gain to ensure highlights are not blowing out and shadows are properly seated.
  • White Balance: Correct color casts so neutral tones are accurate before any stylistic shifts occur.

Making corrections prior to the LUT feeds the formula clean data, preventing color artifacting and harsh clipping.

3. Apply the Creative LUT

Add the creative LUT toward the end of your grading pipeline. This ensures your balanced image receives the intended stylistic look without fighting base-level exposure errors.

4. Adjust LUT Intensity

Most non-linear editors allow you to control the opacity or key output of a LUT. Creative LUTs are often designed aggressively; reducing the LUT’s strength to between 40% and 70% typically yields a more organic and professional result.

5. Execute Secondary Grading

Fine-tune elements downstream of your primary adjustments. If the creative LUT pushes skin tones too far into a specific hue, isolate the skin tones using HSL qualifiers or masks to restore natural color while maintaining the background grade.
